Part 1

Weakness Is a Complete Diagnosis

A weakness is a positional or tactical defect that the opponent can exploit more easily than its owner can remove. Calling something weak is not enough. The defect must be connected to a route of access and a practical conversion method.

Weakness + access + pressure = practical target

Without access, a backward pawn may remain harmless. Without pressure, a weak square may never matter. Without a conversion method, the weakness may tie down pieces but still fail to produce a concrete gain.

Begin with useful questions

  1. What cannot easily be changed?
  2. Which feature can become a stable target?
  3. Which square or file gives access to it?
  4. What freeing move would repair the position?
  5. Can that freeing move be prevented?
  6. Is one weakness enough, or must a second weakness be created?
Part 2

Historical and Strategic Foundations

Wilhelm Steinitz

A successful attack should rest on prior positional advantages. Evaluation comes before aggression.

Aron Nimzowitsch

Blockade, restraint, prophylaxis, overprotection, weak squares, and control of freeing breaks expanded weakness theory beyond pawn targets.

Tigran Petrosian

Prophylaxis is the strategic process of identifying the opponent’s most useful intended move or plan and acting before it happens to prevent it, weaken it, delay it, or make it harmless. The practical question is: If I passed my turn, what would my opponent do next? Typical targets include freeing pawn breaks, favorable exchanges, occupation of an outpost, improvement of a restricted piece, direct threats, and ways to repair a weakness. Prophylaxis is not passive waiting; a strong prophylactic move normally improves your own position while reducing the opponent’s counterplay.

Anatoly Karpov

Small weaknesses can be fixed, increased, and joined by a second target until the defender becomes overloaded.

Static weaknesses

Long-lasting defects such as isolated or backward pawns, permanent holes, bad bishops, fixed targets, and damaged color complexes. They justify patient improvement.

Dynamic weaknesses

Temporary defects such as loose pieces, overloads, pins, poor development, an uncastled king, or a briefly exposed diagonal. They demand immediate action.

Permanent weaknesses

A hole no pawn can challenge or a fixed structural defect may remain for the rest of the game.

Repairable weaknesses

A backward pawn may advance, a bad bishop may be released, or a cramped position may be repaired by a freeing break.

Fix before attacking

A target that can advance, exchange, or escape may not remain weak. Preventing its repair can be more important than attacking it immediately.

Occupy the connected square

The square in front of a backward pawn is often more important than the pawn itself. A protected outpost restricts defenders and makes pressure sustainable.

Create a second weakness

One target may be defendable. A second target on the opposite wing forces the defender to divide pieces and creates the real breakthrough.

Capablanca–Mieses, 1928

Capablanca fixed targets, improved his pieces, limited counterplay, and converted only when the position was ready.

Karpov–Unzicker, 1974

Karpov’s restriction and target-switching provide a classic model of the second-weakness principle.

Part 3

Types of Weaknesses and Their Typical Use

WeaknessDescriptionTypical exploitation
Weak squareA square that cannot be challenged adequately by pawns.Occupy it with a knight or another stable piece.
Backward pawnA pawn unable to advance safely and lacking support from neighboring pawns.Attack it on a semi-open file and occupy the square in front.
Isolated pawnA pawn with no friendly pawn on adjacent files.Blockade it, trade pieces, and enter a favorable endgame.
Doubled pawnsTwo friendly pawns on the same file.Pressure the front pawn or use the weakened adjacent squares.
Hanging pawnsTwo connected pawns without supporting neighbors.Provoke an advance, blockade them, or attack after fixation.
Bad bishopA bishop restricted by its own pawns.Keep the structure fixed and improve the opposing knight.
Weak color complexA group of same-colored squares that is difficult to defend.Exchange the relevant bishop and infiltrate on those squares.
Weak kingDamaged shelter, poor defenders, or limited flight squares.Open lines, remove defenders, and calculate forcing moves.
Loose pieceAn undefended or insufficiently defended piece.Use forks, pins, discovered attacks, and double attacks.
Coordination weaknessPieces obstruct one another or defend poorly.Increase pressure until a defender becomes overloaded.
Space weaknessPieces have too few safe and useful squares.Restrict further and prevent freeing pawn breaks.

Hole

A square in a player’s camp that cannot be defended by a pawn.

Outpost

An advanced square where a piece can operate securely and cannot easily be chased by pawns.

Color complex

A connected group of light or dark squares that one side struggles to control.

Prophylaxis

Anticipating the opponent’s best plan and preventing, weakening, delaying, or neutralizing it before continuing with your own plan. In weakness play, this often means stopping the freeing move that would let a target advance, exchange, or escape.

Freeing break

A pawn move that repairs a cramped or weak position by opening lines, exchanging a target, or activating pieces.

Fixing move

A move that makes a weakness more permanent.

Weakness of the last move

A newly abandoned square, loose piece, weakened diagonal, removed defender, or exposed king created by the opponent’s latest move.

Part 4

The Weakness-Diagnosis Framework

1. Scan

Count material, inspect both kings, and note what changed after the last move.

2. Classify

Separate static from dynamic weaknesses and permanent from repairable defects.

3. Choose access

Find the outpost, file, rank, or diagonal that leads to the target.

4. Stop repair

Identify and prevent the freeing pawn break, exchange, or regrouping.

5. Improve

Bring the best attacking piece to the access square and improve the worst-placed piece.

6. Convert

Increase pressure, create a second weakness when required, and calculate tactical consequences.

Practical decision sequence

  1. Count material and assess king safety.
  2. Identify weak pawns, squares, lines, and pieces.
  3. Compare piece activity and coordination.
  4. Identify the opponent’s best defensive piece and freeing break.
  5. Decide whether the freeing break must be stopped first.
  6. Improve the piece best suited to attack the weakness.
  7. Calculate forcing replies before committing.
Part 5

How to Use Each FEN Position

  1. Load the FEN without showing the solution.
  2. Confirm the side to move.
  3. Describe the position before naming a move.
  4. Give at least two serious candidate moves when the position is not forced.
  5. Calculate a short variation and evaluate the result.
  6. Reveal the model answer only after the strategic idea is explained.
  7. Reset the board and play the position from both sides.
  8. End by stating the transferable rule, not merely the move.

Required explanation

Naming a weak pawn earns only partial credit. The student must also explain the access route, the opponent’s repair, and the next step in the conversion.

Backward Pawn or Outpost?

Learning objective: distinguish the visible target from the square that makes the target exploitable.

White to move

The backward d6-pawn is the visible weakness. The d5 square is the route of access.

Student task

  1. Which black pawn is structurally weak?
  2. Which square gives White access to it?
  3. Can a black pawn challenge that square?
  4. Which white piece should occupy it?
  5. What freeing move would Black like to achieve?

Show model solution
1.Nd5! Bxd5 2.exd5

White does not win d6 immediately. White occupies the hole, forces an exchange, obtains a protected pawn on d5, and keeps the backward d6-pawn under lasting pressure. If Black avoids the exchange, the knight remains on a stable outpost.

Coach explanation

The d6-pawn is the target, but d5 is the operational square. A slow move may allow Black to repair the position with a freeing operation.

Transfer rule

When a pawn is backward, first examine the square directly in front of it.

FEN 2

Create a Weakness with a Minority Attack

Learning objective: understand that a weakness may need to be created before it can be attacked.

White to move

White’s queenside minority can challenge Black’s larger pawn group.

Student task

  1. Which wing contains White’s pawn minority?
  2. Which advance challenges Black’s majority?
  3. What weakness is White trying to create?
  4. Why is the plan not simply to win a pawn?
1.b4
1.e4
1.Bd3
Show model line
1.b4 a5 2.b5 cxb5 3.Bxb5

The exact line depends on Black’s defense. White’s stable aim is to force exchanges or advances, create a backward or isolated pawn, open files for the rooks, and gain queenside entry squares.

Coach explanation

A minority attack is not a pawn race. White uses fewer pawns to damage a larger pawn group. Advancing before the pieces can use the opened files is a common error.

Transfer rule

A pawn break is justified by the structure it creates, not merely by the space it gains.

FEN 3

A Bad Bishop Is a Network Weakness

Learning objective: evaluate a restricted bishop together with the pawn structure and the opposing knight.

White to move

Black’s bishop is restricted by its own pawn structure while White’s knight can seek stable squares.

Student task

  1. Which black piece has the least useful mobility?
  2. Which pawns restrict it?
  3. Which white minor piece benefits from the closed center?
  4. Should White open the center immediately?
  5. Find a knight-improving maneuver.
Show model plan
1.Ne2 b5 2.Nf4

The knight approaches useful central and kingside squares while Black’s bishop remains restricted. Opening the position without preparation could repair Black’s problem.

Coach explanation

The weakness is not the bishop by itself. It is the relationship among the bishop, Black’s fixed pawns, White’s knight outposts, and the closed center.

Transfer rule

Preserve the structure that makes your good piece good and the opponent’s bad piece bad.

FEN 4

Exploit a Temporary King Weakness

Learning objective: distinguish a temporary dynamic weakness from a lasting structural weakness.

White to move

Black’s king remains in the center but may castle on the next move.

Student task

  1. Is the king weakness permanent?
  2. Which move opens the center before Black castles?
  3. Why would a slow improving move miss the moment?
  4. Calculate the first exchange.
Show model solution
1.d4! exd4 2.Nxd4

White uses the temporary placement of Black’s king to open central lines and accelerate development. The point is not a forced win; it is acting before the weakness disappears after castling.

Coach explanation

Static weaknesses reward patience. Dynamic weaknesses punish delay. A useful-looking move such as h3 or Re1 may simply allow Black to castle.

Transfer rule

Exploit a temporary weakness before the opponent repairs it.

FEN 5

The Second Weakness

Learning objective: switch targets when the defender is tied to the first weakness.

White to move

Black’s king is close to a6 but too far from the h6-pawn.

Student task

  1. Which pawn is currently defended by the king?
  2. Which pawn cannot be defended in time?
  3. What rook move switches the attack?
  4. Why is attacking a6 directly less effective?
Show model solution
1.Rh4! Kb5 2.Rxh6

Black’s king can remain near one weakness but cannot defend both wings. White switches faster than the king can reorganize.

Coach explanation

The first weakness fixes the defender. The distant second weakness creates the breakthrough. Continuing to attack a6 with every piece would merely help Black defend efficiently.

Transfer rule

When one target is fully defended, switch to a distant second target.

Part 7

Misconceptions, Errors, and Practical Use

Attack immediately?

Direct pressure may let the target advance or disappear. Improving pieces or stopping the freeing move may come first.

Only pawns are weak?

Squares, color complexes, kings, pieces, files, diagonals, development, and coordination can all be weak.

Isolated means bad?

An isolated pawn may provide space, open lines, active pieces, and attacking chances. Its weakness grows after exchanges remove those benefits.

A square matters alone?

A weak square matters only when it can be occupied or used to support another operation.

Typical errors

  • Attacking the wrong target.
  • Confusing a temporary tactical defect with a lasting strategic weakness.
  • Allowing the opponent’s freeing break.
  • Exchanging the best piece for exploiting the target.
  • Opening the position for the opponent’s better bishop.
  • Adding attackers without improving access.
  • Ignoring king safety during a positional operation.
  • Failing to calculate active defensive resources.

During the opponent’s turn

Evaluate possible improvements, weaknesses on both sides, available pawn breaks, loose pieces, and the targets likely to survive exchanges.

During opening study

Record which pawn move creates a hole, which side owns the better minor piece, which freeing break each side needs, and which endgame favors the structure.

During game analysis

Mark when a weakness was created, when it could have been fixed, when a freeing move was allowed, and when a second weakness could have been created.

Exercise 1: What is Black’s main long-term weakness in FEN 1?

The backward d6-pawn is the visible target, but d5 is the key strategic square. Occupying or controlling d5 makes pressure against d6 effective.

Exercise 2: Which plan is thematic in FEN 2?

Queenside structural pressure through the minority attack. White seeks to create a long-term weakness in Black’s pawn majority.

Exercise 3: Which minor-piece comparison favors White in FEN 3?

White’s knight is superior to Black’s restricted bishop. White should keep the structure fixed enough for the knight’s outposts to remain useful.

Exercise 4: Apply the framework to one of your own games.

Identify one static weakness, one dynamic weakness, one freeing break, the piece that should attack or defend the weakness, and one possible second weakness.

Mastery standard

  3. Name the opponent’s repair or freeing move.
  4. Choose a piece suited to the target.
