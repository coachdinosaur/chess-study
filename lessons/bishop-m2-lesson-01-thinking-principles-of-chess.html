Thinking Principles of Chess

A practical five-part thinking system for choosing moves: captures, weaknesses, threats, development, and the opponent’s last move.

1. Capture 2. Weakness 3. Threat 4. Development 5. Last Move Review
1. CaptureAvoid losing material and calculate chances to gain material.
2. WeaknessAvoid creating weaknesses and plan to exploit the opponent’s weaknesses.
3. ThreatNotice urgent threats and search for forcing threats of your own.
4. DevelopmentBring pieces into useful, coordinated positions.
5. Last MoveAsk what changed and why the opponent chose that move.
Principle 1

Capture

The capture principle means avoiding unnecessary material losses and looking for sound ways to gain material. It does not mean capturing automatically. Every capture still has to survive calculation.

Position 1 · Black to move

The knight on f5 is attacked by the bishop on d3.

Black may retreat the knight to h6 or e7, but either retreat leaves the h7-pawn vulnerable. Black can instead support the knight.

Compare the defenses

Queen support: 1...Qf6 keeps the knight defended, but the queen can become exposed and Black still loses pawns.

Pawn support: 1...g6 supports the knight more naturally and is the better practical choice in the source lesson.

Principle 2

Weakness

Avoid weaknesses in your own position and make plans that exploit weaknesses in the opponent’s position.

Common weaknesses

  1. Unprotected pieces
  2. Uncoordinated pieces
  3. Isolated or doubled pawns
  4. An unsafe king
  5. Lack of space
  6. A blocked bishop
  7. Uncentralized knights
  8. Tactical weaknesses
  9. Weak squares, files, or diagonals

Position 3 · Black to move

Black should preserve activity instead of placing the rook in a cramped position.

The source compares 1...Ra8 with 1...Rc6. The move 1...Ra8 reduces the rook’s space and activity. The recommended move is 1...Rc6, keeping the rook active and the position balanced.

Do not solve one problem by creating another

A move that saves material can still be inferior if it leaves a piece passive or cramped.

Principle 3

Threat

Look for the opponent’s threats and stop them when necessary. At the same time, search for a stronger threat of your own, especially forcing checks, captures, and mating ideas.

Position 5 · White to move

Black threatens mate, but White has a forcing mating attack.

White can capture the knight on d3, but Black then uses forcing queen checks and wins substantial material.

The stronger solution is to ignore passive defense and begin a direct mating attack with 1.Rxg6+.

Practical threat rule

When the opponent threatens something serious, do not defend automatically. First check whether you have a forcing sequence that is more urgent.

Principle 4

Development

Development means bringing pieces into useful positions in a coordinated way so that they can help with attack, defense, central control, and king safety.

Position 6 · White to move

White should open lines and activate the queenside pieces.

The source recommends a strategic plan rather than a forced move:

  • Open the c-file.
  • Activate the rook on a1.
  • Improve the knights toward useful central squares.
  • Coordinate the pieces before starting operations elsewhere.

Development is not merely movement

A piece is properly developed when it has a useful job and cooperates with the rest of the army.

Principle 5

Last Move of the Opponent

Analyze why the opponent made the last move. Every move changes attacks, defenses, lines, weak squares, and future plans.

Five-Principle Move Checklist

Use this sequence before every important move:

  • Capture: What is attacked, loose, or available to capture?
  • Weakness: What weaknesses exist on both sides?
  • Threat: What is the opponent threatening, and do I have a stronger threat?
  • Development: Which piece is least useful and needs improvement?
  • Last move: What changed, and what does the opponent intend?
Review

Check Your Understanding

1. Does the capture principle mean that every available capture should be played?

No. It means captures should be considered and calculated. The final position must justify the capture.

2. Why is 1...Rc6 preferred to 1...Ra8 in Position 3?

Rc6 keeps the rook active and avoids the space weakness and passivity created by Ra8.

3. What makes 1...Nxb2 especially useful in Position 4?

Black wins a pawn and attacks the bishop on d3 with tempo.

4. What is the main lesson of the mating attack in Position 5?

Before defending an enemy threat, check whether you have a stronger forcing sequence of your own.

5. What should you ask after every opponent move?

Ask what changed, what the moved piece attacks or stopped defending, and what plan the move suggests.
