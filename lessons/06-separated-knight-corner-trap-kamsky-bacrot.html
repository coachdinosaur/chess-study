Ending 9

Rook vs Knight — Separation, Corner Trap & Kamsky-Bacrot

The separated knight becomes the target, corner trap patterns that are theoretically lost, the knight's dumb square, and a practical example from elite chess showing how difficult the defence really is.

  • Understand why a separated knight is the rook's primary target.
  • Follow the trapping process: restrict, take away escapes, bring the king.
  • Recognise corner trap configurations as theoretically lost.
  • Know the knight's dumb square on g2 and why it is fatal.
  • Appreciate that practical defence can be extremely difficult even for elite players.
  • Understand zugzwang as the final winning mechanism in Rook vs Knight.
Analysis

The Knight Becomes the Target

Once the knight becomes separated from its king, the stronger side's main objective is to trap it. The trapping process usually involves restricting the knight's mobility, taking away escape squares, using pins and double attacks, and bringing the king closer.

Position 1.20 · Rook vs Knight: Separated Knight · Black to move

FEN: 8/8/8/3N4/8/5k2/2r5/4K3 b - - 0 4

The white knight on d5 has moved away from the king on e1 and is very likely to be lost. The black rook on c2 and king on f3 are well placed to begin the hunt.

Main line

4...Rc4 5.Nb6 Rb4 6.Nc8 Rb7! 7.Kd2 Kf4 8.Ke2 Ke5 -+

The rook gradually removes every escape square until the king can capture the knight.

Variation — 5.Nf6

5.Nf6 Rd4 6.Nh7 Kf4 7.Nf8 Kf5 +-

Variation — 6.Nd5

6.Nd5 Re4+ 7.Kf1 Rd4 8.Nc3 Rd3 +-

Variation — 7.Nd6

7.Nd6 Re7+ 8.Kf1 Rd7 +-

Practical Rule

When defending Rook vs Knight, never allow the knight to become separated from its king.

Analysis

Temporary Separation

Continuation from the previous position. There is one case where a temporary separation is acceptable.

Main line

4...Re2+ 5.Kf1 Rh2 6.Ke1 7.Kf1 =

The king immediately returns beside the knight. This is the only acceptable temporary separation.

Key Idea

A temporary separation is acceptable only if king and knight reunite immediately.

Rook vs Knight in the Corner

Everything changes once both defending pieces are trapped near the corner. Mobility disappears, and the knight is normally lost.

Lesson

Corner Trap Pattern

There is no need for lengthy calculation. This configuration is theoretically lost.

Position 1.21 · Corner trap pattern · White to move

FEN: 8/8/8/8/8/6k1/r7/6NK w - - 0 1

The white knight on g1 and king on h1 are trapped near the corner. The black rook on a2 and king on g3 have full control. White loses the knight immediately.

Key Idea

When king and knight are trapped near the corner with no mobility, the position is theoretically lost. The rook wins without needing lengthy calculation.

Lesson

Both Pieces in the Corner

Both defending pieces are trapped in the corner, so the knight is lost.

Position 1.22 · Both pieces in the corner · White to move

FEN: 8/8/8/8/8/6k1/r7/6KN w - - 0 1

With the king on g1 and the knight on h1, both pieces are trapped. The rook easily wins once both defending pieces have no room to manoeuvre.

Key Idea

The rook easily wins once both defending pieces have no room. Corner imprisonment is fatal regardless of how the pieces are arranged within it.

Lesson

The Knight's Dumb Square

Even with the knight on g2, it is lost because both pieces are trapped. This is the first appearance of the knight's dumb square.

Position 1.23 · The knight's dumb square · White to move

FEN: r7/8/8/8/8/6k1/6N1/7K w - - 0 1

The knight on g2 is not on the edge itself, but it is still trapped because of the surrounding geometry. The king on h1 has no escape, and the rook on a8 controls everything.

Key Idea

The square g2 is a notorious “dumb square” for the knight in these endings. Although the knight is not on the edge itself, it is still trapped because of the surrounding geometry.

Summary

Practical Summary

The two defensive rules to remember from this section:

The Two Defensive Rules

  1. Never separate the knight from its king.
  2. Never allow both king and knight to become trapped in the corner, even if the knight is on g2.
Practical Example

Kamsky vs Bacrot (Sofia 2006)

Bacrot promoted to a knight, reaching what is generally considered a theoretical draw. However, the knight is dangerously close to its dumb square, making defence much harder than expected.

Position 1.24 · Kamsky vs Bacrot, Sofia 2006 · White to move

FEN: 8/8/8/8/8/5K2/7R/4nk2 w - - 0 1 · Bacrot has just promoted to a knight.

The position looks like a standard draw, but the knight on e1 is dangerously close to the dumb square g2. Defence requires extreme precision.

Main game — forced phase

1.Kg3 Nd3 2.Rd2 Ne1 3.Rf2+ Kg1 4.Rf8 Ng2

Up to this point the moves are essentially forced. Now Black faces a critical decision.

Candidate Moves at Move 4...Black to Move

  • 4...Nd3? loses.
  • 4...Nc2 draws and is the easiest defence.
  • 4...Ng2 is playable but leads to difficult defence.

Game continuation

5.Kf3 Kf1?

This is the losing move.

Correct defence

5...Nh4+ 6.Ke3 Kg2 7.Rg8+ Kh3 8.Kf2 Kh2 =

This returns to a drawable setup.

Missed Winning Opportunity

Kamsky also missed an earlier winning opportunity with 6.Rf7! instead of the game continuation.

6.Rf7! Kg1 7.Kf3 Kf1? 8.Rf7!

White reaches a remarkable zugzwang.

Lesson

Zugzwang Position

An amazing zugzwang position after 11.Rh7. Black is to move and has no good option.

Position 1.25 · Zugzwang after 11.Rh7 · Black to move

FEN: 8/7R/8/8/8/8/4K1n1/6k1 b - - 0 11

The knight on g2 — the dumb square — has almost no safe moves. The king on g1 is boxed in. Black is in complete zugzwang.

Principal variation

11...Nf4+ 12.Kf3 Nd3 13.Rh4 Ne5+ 14.Ke2?

This misses the strongest continuation.

Better

14.Ke3! +-

Game continuation

14...Kg2 15.Re4 Nf7?

15...Nd7! would have drawn.

Remaining moves

16.Re7 Nd6 17.Rg7+ Kh3 18.Kf3 Kh4 19.Kf4 Kh5 20.Re7 Nc4 21.Re6 Nd2 22.Rc6 Nb3 23.Ke3 Kg4 24.Rc4+ Kg3 25.Rc3 Na5 26.Ke4+ Kf2 27.Kd5 Nb7 28.Rb3 Nd8 29.Rb8 1-0

Final Practical Lessons

  • Theory provides the framework.
  • Practical defence can still be extremely difficult.
  • The knight's dumb square and king placement can completely change the evaluation.
  • Even elite players can fail to find the only drawing continuation under tournament conditions.

Chapter Checklist

  • A separated knight is the rook's primary target — never allow it.
  • The trapping process restricts mobility, takes away escapes, and brings the king closer.
  • A temporary separation is acceptable only if king and knight reunite immediately.
  • Corner trap configurations with both pieces trapped are theoretically lost.
  • The knight's dumb square on g2 is fatal despite not being on the edge.
  • Practical defence is far harder than theory suggests — even elite players fail.
  • Zugzwang is the final winning mechanism when the knight is on its dumb square.
