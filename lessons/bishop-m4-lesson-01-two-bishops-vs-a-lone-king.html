Part 2

Keys to Victory

The technique is systematic. The bishops form a movable fence, and the king helps push that fence forward.

Five essential rules

  1. Create a diagonal fence. Use connected diagonals to stop the king from crossing.
  2. Take away extra squares. Each bishop should restrict more than one route.
  3. Shrink the territory. Coordinate king and bishops as in the rook box method.
  4. Drive the king to a corner. The final mating pattern occurs at the edge.
  5. Protect both bishops. Never allow the defending king to capture either one.

Choose the Correct Waiting Move

This is the critical moment. A correct waiting move preserves a legal square for Black and prepares the final check.

The Final Checkmate

The bishops and king now cover every flight square around a8.

Two-Bishop Mate Checklist

Use this sequence whenever you have two bishops and a king against a lone king:

  • Fence: Are the bishops controlling neighboring diagonals?
  • Protect: Can the enemy king capture either bishop?
  • Approach: Is my king moving closer without opening an escape route?
  • Shrink: Which bishop move removes another rank, file, or diagonal of space?
  • Corner: Can I force the king toward a corner safely?
  • Verify: Does my waiting move avoid stalemate?
Review

Check Your Understanding

1. Why do the bishops work best on connected diagonals?

They create a barrier the enemy king cannot cross, allowing White to shrink the king’s territory gradually.

2. Why must the attacking king approach?

The king controls close escape squares and supports the bishops in the final mating net.

3. What is the purpose of 12.Be5?

It prevents the Black king from escaping through b8.

4. Why is 17.Bd4?? a draw?

It leaves the Black king with no legal move while the king is not in check, so the position is stalemate.

5. What is the final mating move?

19.Bd5#.
