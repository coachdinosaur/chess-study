Part 1

Calculation Skill Versus Calculation Process

Calculation is the skill of visualizing and comparing variations. The calculation process is the repeatable workflow used to apply that skill during a game.

Update → safety → evaluation → candidates → calculation → comparison → final check

A disciplined process reduces random analysis, tunnel vision, missed threats, repeated branches, premature commitment, overlooked defensive resources, and one-move blunders. It must remain flexible: the purpose is to organize attention, not to force a ceremony onto every trivial position.

Finding weaknesses

Tells the player where to look and which defects may justify calculation.

Positional play

Defines the transformation or future position the player wants.

Defense

Supplies blocks, exchanges, counterplay, material returns, and drawing resources.

Calculation

Visualizes branches, finds best replies, and compares stable endpoints.

Part 2

From Kotov's Tree to a Flexible Workflow

Kotov's analysis tree

Candidate moves, separated branches, disciplined calculation, and avoidance of repeated analysis made chess thinking explicit.

Later refinements

Trainers emphasized visualization, endpoint evaluation, comparison, and the fact that one branch may reveal a motif relevant to another.

Modern practice

Last-move awareness, threat detection, flexible candidate generation, forcing-move priority, positional evaluation, comparison, and final verification work together.

Disciplined feedback

Branches should remain organized, but new tactical information may legitimately create a candidate or change the evaluation.

Part 3

The Full Ten-Stage Process

  1. Position update. Identify what moved, what it stopped defending, what it attacks, which lines opened or closed, which squares weakened, and whether a piece became loose.
  2. Safety check. Before planning, scan for mate, material loss, tactical shots, passed-pawn breakthroughs, forks, pins, discovered attacks, and removal of a key defender.
  3. Evaluation. Judge king safety, material, immediate tactics, development, activity, pawn structure, weak squares and files, minor-piece quality, space, initiative, and endgame prospects.
  4. Candidate generation. Select a small set of forcing, defensive, positional, and transformational moves, each with a concrete reason.
  5. Prioritization. Usually begin with forced replies, checks, forcing captures, direct threats, defensive resources, pawn breaks, and then quiet moves. Urgency overrides category.
  6. Concrete calculation. Make the candidate mentally, find the opponent's strongest reply, continue forcing play, track every piece, check legality, and watch for move-order changes.
  7. Endpoint evaluation. At a stable point, assess material, king safety, activity, pawn structure, passed pawns, weak squares, initiative, endgame result, and practical difficulty.
  8. Candidate comparison. Compare the best-defended outcome of every serious move, then consider execution difficulty, flexibility, time, and unnecessary risk.
  9. Selection. Choose the move with the best result against the strongest response, not the first attractive move or the line that feels easiest to imagine.
  10. Final blunder check. Visualize the move as played and scan opponent checks, captures, threats, loose pieces, king lines, intermediate moves, and legality.

Part 5

Managing the Analysis Tree and the Clock

Avoid branch jumping

Finish a critical branch to a stable endpoint unless an immediate refutation makes further work unnecessary.

Avoid repeated calculation

Record a branch conclusion mentally: loses material, equalizes, draws, gives an unclear attack, or reaches a winning ending.

Use elimination

Reject moves that lose by force, permit mate, repeat a worse version of another candidate, or fail to the same tactical reply.

Allow intelligent feedback

If a branch reveals a motif that affects another candidate, update the analysis. Structure should prevent chaos, not learning.

Spend time on critical moments

Captures, pawn breaks, sacrifices, major exchanges, king-safety choices, and irreversible structural changes deserve more time.

Move faster when safe

Forced moves, familiar positions, obvious safe replies, and positions without tactical or structural transformation need less time.

No position updateThe player follows an old plan and misses the point of the opponent's move.
One-candidate thinkingThe first attractive move receives all the calculation.
Cooperative opponentThe expected reply replaces the strongest reply.
Premature endpointThe player evaluates while forcing checks, captures, or threats remain.
No evaluationA long line is calculated without judging the resulting position.
No comparisonOne good move is found, but a better one is never examined.

Part 7

Training the Process

Written candidates

Write the candidates before calculating so omissions and tunnel vision become visible.

Verbal protocol

Explain what changed, the threat, the evaluation, the candidates, and the calculated result.

Timed positions

Use fixed limits and record the stage at which the process broke down.

Error classification

Label mistakes as update, threat, candidate, calculation, visualization, evaluation, comparison, blunder-check, or time-management errors.

Multiple time limits

Solve the same position in 30 seconds, 3 minutes, and 10 minutes, then compare candidates and depth.

Post-game correction

Record the move played, the process failure, and the corrected workflow rather than only storing an engine number.

Part 8

How to Teach the FEN Positions

  1. Load the FEN without showing the answer.
  2. Confirm the side to move and the opponent's last move when supplied.
  3. Ask what changed before requesting a candidate.
  4. Require at least two serious candidates unless the position is forced.
  5. Ask for the opponent's strongest reply and a short line.
  6. Require an endpoint evaluation before revealing the model.
  7. Reset the position and let the student test another branch.
  8. Finish with the transferable process rule.

Laboratory 5

Defensive Candidate Generation

Learning objective: Include blocks and threat-neutralizing moves before calculating attractive counterplay.

Position 5 · White to move

The knight on g4 is visible; the queen-and-knight pressure on h2 is the real threat.

Process task

  1. Update: what changed when the knight reached g4?
  2. Identify the queen's diagonal and the mating square.
  3. Compare 1.h3, 1.g3, and 1.Bf4.
  4. Which candidate neutralizes the threat at its source?
h3?
g3!
Bf4
Reveal the model comparison

1.h3? attacks the knight but weakens the king and allows ideas based on ...Nd4. 1.g3! blocks the queen's diagonal and addresses the real threat.

Coach explanation

Defensive positions require candidate categories such as blocks, exchanges, simplification, and threat-neutralizing moves. Searching only for counterattacks is an incomplete process.

Process failure exposed

Reacting to the nearest attacking piece instead of the coordinated mating threat.

Transfer rule

In danger, generate defensive candidates before attractive active moves.

Laboratory 6

Stage 10: The Final Blunder Check

Learning objective: Visualize the selected move as played and perform a fresh one-move opponent scan.

Position 6 · Black to move · Deep Fritz–Kramnik, Bonn 2006

Black is considering 34...Qe3. A final scan of White's checks ends the calculation immediately.

Final-check questions

  1. Place Black's queen mentally on e3.
  2. List every White check.
  3. Does Black's king have an escape square?
  4. Can Black capture or block the checking piece?
Qe3??
Kg8
Qb6
Reveal the model line
34...Qe3?? 35.Qh7#

Qh7 is mate. The white queen is protected by the knight on f8, and Black's king has no legal answer. A safer king move was available.

Coach explanation

Deep calculation does not replace the final one-move safety scan. The selected move changes the board, so the opponent's checks, captures, and threats must be inspected again from that new position.

Process failure exposed

Completing complex analysis and moving without a fresh opponent-check scan.

Transfer rule

After choosing a move, visualize it as played and scan every opponent check, capture, and direct threat.

Review

Chapter Summary and Process Mastery

The complete calculation process

  1. Update the position after the opponent's move.
  2. Check safety and identify threats.
  3. Evaluate the position.
  4. Generate candidates with clear reasons.
  5. Prioritize critical branches.
  6. Calculate the opponent's best defense.
  7. Stop at a stable endpoint.
  8. Evaluate and compare the results.
  9. Select the best move.
  10. Perform a final blunder check.

A strong process does not guarantee perfection. It makes mistakes visible, calculation organized, and improvement measurable.

What proves process mastery?

The student updates after every opponent move, identifies safety issues before planning, generates candidates from evaluation, calculates the strongest reply, stops only at a stable endpoint, and performs a final check before moving.

When should the full process be shortened?

When the move is forced, familiar, clearly safe, or the position contains no meaningful tactical or structural transformation. The workflow must scale with criticality.
