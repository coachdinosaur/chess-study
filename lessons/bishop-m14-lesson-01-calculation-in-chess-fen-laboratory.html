Part 1

Calculation Is Selective Concreteness

Calculation is the disciplined process of generating serious candidate moves, analyzing the opponent’s strongest replies, following relevant branches to stable endpoints, and comparing the resulting positions.

Candidates → best defense → forcing continuation → stable endpoint → evaluation → comparison

Accuracy matters more than raw depth. Not every legal move deserves analysis, not every position requires a long tactical tree, and the first forcing move is not automatically best. A move may be good and still be inferior to another candidate.

Calculation is not random visualization

The purpose is not to see the greatest number of moves. It is to spend attention on the branches that can change the evaluation.

Part 2

Historical Development

Early instruction

Calculation was taught mainly through combinations and recurring tactical motifs.

Alexander Kotov

Candidate moves and the analysis tree gave players a systematic language for organizing branches.

Later trainers

Visualization, endpoint evaluation, comparison, and error diagnosis became central rather than merely calculating deeper.

Modern refinement

Strong calculation is flexible: forcing moves receive priority, quiet candidates remain possible, and search depth depends on the position.

Mikhail Tal

Creative candidate generation and forcing complications, backed by practical judgment.

Garry Kasparov

Deep concrete calculation joined to initiative, preparation, and dynamic evaluation.

Anatoly Karpov

Precise calculation of quiet restrictions, exchanges, and positional transformations.

Modern grandmasters

Calculation and evaluation are integrated continuously; tactics, strategy, and defense are not separate mental departments.

Part 3

Fundamental Principles

Generate candidates first

Include forcing moves, strategically critical moves, defensive resources, and quiet moves that change the opponent’s plan.

Calculate best defense

Do not assume the opponent recaptures, accepts a sacrifice, or follows the most obvious line.

Prioritize forcing moves

Checks, captures, and threats reduce replies, but forcing appearance does not guarantee quality.

Detect intermediate moves

A check, capture, attack on the queen, or stronger threat may interrupt the expected recapture sequence.

Reach a stable endpoint

Stop when forcing moves have settled and the resulting position can be evaluated reliably.

Compare, then verify

Evaluate every serious candidate, choose provisionally, and perform a final blunder check before moving.

Part 5

Calculation Framework

  1. Identify tactical and strategic features
  2. Generate serious candidates
  3. Prioritize forcing candidates
  4. Calculate the opponent’s best reply
  5. Continue the forcing sequence
  6. Check for intermediate moves
  7. Stop at a stable endpoint
  8. Evaluate material and king safety
  9. Evaluate activity, structure, and initiative
  10. Calculate the next candidate
  11. Compare final positions
  12. Choose a provisional move
  13. Run a final blunder check
  14. Play only after verification
Part 6

Common Errors and Practical Methods

Typical errors

Calculating only one candidate, assuming cooperation, stopping too early, counting material while ignoring king safety, repeating branches, missing intermediate moves, forgetting defensive resources, failing to compare candidates, and overcalculating irrelevant lines.

Final-move error

A correct analysis can still be ruined by moving before checking checks, captures, threats, loose pieces, and changes caused by the opponent’s last move.

Useful exercise forms

Candidate lists, no-moving-board calculation, guess-the-move, defensive positions, pawn races, endgame calculation, and reconstruction of variations from memory.

Error log

Classify mistakes as candidate omission, visualization, evaluation, opponent-response, premature stopping, or time-management errors.

Part 7

How to Teach the FEN Positions

  1. Load the FEN without revealing the answer.
  2. Confirm the side to move and describe the position.
  3. Require at least two serious candidates.
  4. Ask for the opponent’s strongest reply.
  5. Continue forcing moves until a stable endpoint.
  6. Evaluate the endpoint before revealing the model.
  7. Reset and let the student calculate another candidate.
  8. Finish with the transferable calculation rule.
Laboratory 1

Do Not Stop After Winning the Queen

Learning objective: Use intermediate moves, complete the forcing branch, and evaluate only at a stable endpoint.

Position 1 · White to move

White can apparently win Black’s queen on d8, but Black has an intermediate check that changes the result.

Student task

  1. After 1.Nxd5, what is Black’s strongest recapture?
  2. After 2.Bxd8, which forcing move must Black play before recapturing the bishop?
  3. Where must White answer the check?
  4. What is the final material result after the entire sequence?
Nxd5
e3
Nf3
Reveal the model calculation
1.Nxd5?? Nxd5 2.Bxd8 Bb4+! 3.Qd2 Bxd2+ 4.Kxd2 Kxd8

White does not win Black’s queen. Black inserts ...Bb4+ before recapturing on d8 and completes the forcing sequence.

Coach explanation

Calculation often fails because the player stops when the desired result first appears. A branch is not finished while forcing checks, captures, or intermediate moves remain.

Common error

Evaluating the line immediately after 2.Bxd8 and declaring the queen won.

Transfer rule

Before evaluating a line, finish every forcing check, capture, and intermediate move.

Laboratory 2

Underpromotion as a Calculation Candidate

Learning objective: Generate every promotion choice and value forcing tempo over nominal material.

Position 2 · Black to move

Black’s advanced pawn can capture with check and then promote. The automatic queen is not the only candidate.

Student task

  1. Find Black’s forcing pawn capture.
  2. What is White’s king response?
  3. List all four legal promotion choices.
  4. Which promotion gives check and preserves the initiative?
fxg1=Q
fxg1=R
fxg1=N+
Reveal the model calculation
1...exf2+ 2.Ke2 fxg1=N+!

The knight promotion gives check, wins material, and keeps White under immediate pressure.

Coach explanation

Candidate generation applies to promotion pieces too. Promote to a queen is a useful default, not a law of nature.

Common error

Considering only promotion to a queen and missing a checking underpromotion.

Transfer rule

At promotion, calculate queen, rook, bishop, and knight whenever a check, fork, or stalemate detail may matter.

Laboratory 3

Count the Race, Then Check the Promotion

Learning objective: Calculate exact tempi and inspect the position after promotion, especially checks.

Position 3 · White to move

Both pawns are close to promotion. Counting moves is necessary, but the checking promotion decides the sequence.

Student task

  1. How many moves does each pawn need to promote?
  2. Does either promotion give check?
  3. Why is move-counting alone insufficient?
  4. Calculate White’s first move and Black’s forced king response.
h8=Q+
Kd2
h8=N
Reveal the model calculation
1.h8=Q+! Ka2

White promotes with check. Black must answer the check before advancing the b-pawn.

Coach explanation

Pawn races require more than my pawn queens first. The final move may give check, attack the opposing pawn, allow a skewer, create stalemate, or justify underpromotion.

Common error

Counting promotion tempi without checking the board after promotion.

Transfer rule

In every pawn race, calculate the position after promotion, not merely the number of moves to promotion.

Laboratory 4

Defensive Calculation Finds the Quiet Move

Learning objective: Prevent the opponent’s attacking setup instead of reacting one move later.

Position 4 · White to move

Black wants ...Bc5 and pressure against f2. White’s strongest candidate prevents several ideas at once.

Student task

  1. What is Black’s intended developing move?
  2. Compare 1.d3, 1.d4, and 1.Be2.
  3. Which move closes the bishop’s diagonal and restricts the e4-knight?
  4. Why is merely chasing the knight less accurate?
d3
d4!
Be2
Reveal the model calculation
1.d4!

White occupies the center, prevents ...Bc5, supports the e5-pawn, and reduces Black’s tactical possibilities in one move.

Coach explanation

Calculation includes the opponent’s intended setup, not only immediate checks and captures. A quiet move can prevent several coordinated ideas.

Common error

Reacting to the knight alone and overlooking Black’s developing attack against f2.

Transfer rule

Calculate what the opponent wants to play next, then search for moves that prevent several ideas at once.

Laboratory 5

A Quiet Move Can Be the Best Tactical Decision

Learning objective: Decline tactical bait by evaluating the lines opened after a capture.

Position 5 · White to move

The pawn on g4 looks available, but opening the h-file gives Black’s attack the line it needs.

Student task

  1. What happens to the h-file after hxg4?
  2. Where can Black’s queen enter?
  3. Which quiet move continues development and declines the sacrifice?
  4. Why is refusing material a calculation result rather than fear?
hxg4?
d3!
Nc3
Reveal the model calculation
1.d3! (1.hxg4? hxg4)

White keeps the h-file closed and continues development. The capture gives Black an open attacking line and dangerous queen-entry possibilities.

Coach explanation

The correct question is not only Can I take it? but What position exists after I take it? Material counting without line evaluation is unfinished calculation.

Common error

Accepting the pawn because it appears free while ignoring the opened h-file and king danger.

Transfer rule

Evaluate the opened lines after a capture, not only the material gained.

Laboratory 6

Positional Calculation Has Concrete Branches

Learning objective: Compare a positional candidate with slower alternatives and evaluate the resulting structure.

Position 6 · White to move

White’s best candidate changes the pawn structure. The endpoint is quiet, but it must still be calculated accurately.

Student task

  1. Compare 1.Nd5, 1.Be3, and 1.h3.
  2. What is Black’s strongest reply to each?
  3. Which candidate prevents ...d5 and improves access to d6?
  4. What pawn structure remains after the exchanges?
Nd5!
Be3
h3
Reveal the model calculation
1.Nd5! Bxd5 2.exd5

White accepts a structural transformation because the resulting position fixes Black’s weakness, controls key squares, and preserves long-term pressure.

Coach explanation

Positional calculation may finish in a quiet position. The endpoint still requires visualization of the pawn structure, piece quality, access squares, and future breaks.

Common error

Rejecting a positional candidate because it does not produce an immediate tactical gain.

Transfer rule

Calculate positional moves until the new structure can be evaluated clearly.

Review

Chapter Summary and Mastery Check

Calculation is selective concreteness

  1. Generate more than one serious candidate.
  2. Calculate the opponent’s strongest defense.
  3. Finish checks, captures, threats, and intermediate moves.
  4. Stop at a stable endpoint.
  5. Evaluate the resulting position.
  6. Compare candidates and verify the final choice.
Why is the first forcing move not automatically best?

Because forcing character limits replies but does not guarantee that the resulting endpoint is favorable. Quiet or defensive candidates may produce a better position.

When should a calculation branch stop?

At a stable endpoint where immediate forcing play has settled enough to evaluate material, king safety, activity, structure, and initiative.

What proves mastery?

The student can generate multiple candidates, find the opponent’s strongest defense, detect intermediate moves, calculate to a stable endpoint, and evaluate the final position rather than admiring the variation.
