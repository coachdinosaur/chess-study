Part 1

What to Do After You Move

During the opponent’s turn, do not merely wait for a blunder and do not spend all your time guessing one exact reply.

Use the opponent’s time for two jobs

  1. Evaluate strengths and weaknesses. Scan both armies, both kings, and the pawn structure.
  2. Plan development. Decide which piece needs improvement and which weakness deserves pressure.

Do not confuse evaluation with prediction

A weakness scan prepares you for several possible replies. Calculating only the move you hope the opponent will play prepares you for disappointment.

The Isolated Pawn

An isolated pawn has no friendly pawn on either neighboring file. It often requires piece protection and can become a long-term target.

Position 4 · The isolated e6-pawn · PDF page 9

The e6-pawn has no friendly pawn on the d- or f-file.

White can increase pressure on e6, remove its defenders, or occupy useful squares around it.

Structural weakness, not instant prize

A weak pawn may require several improving moves before it can be won.

Part 6

Unsafe King

Open files, diagonals, weak pawn cover, and coordinated attackers can turn the king into the most urgent weakness on the board.

King-safety warning signs

  • A king that remains in the center.
  • Open files or diagonals leading toward the king.
  • Missing or advanced pawns around the king.
  • Defenders that are pinned, overloaded, or far away.
  • Several enemy pieces already aimed at the same area.

Position 3 demonstrates the principle: White’s queen and bishop enter through open lines while the rooks and pawns support the attack.

Weakness-Scanning Routine

Use this sequence while the opponent is thinking:

  • Loose units: Which pieces or pawns have no defender?
  • Coordination: Which pieces are active, blocked, isolated, or far from the important area?
  • Pawn structure: Which pawns are isolated, doubled, backward, or overextended?
  • King safety: Which files and diagonals lead toward either king?
  • Weak lines: Which squares, files, or diagonals can be occupied?
  • Exploitability: Which weakness can be attacked without creating a worse weakness?
Review

Check Your Understanding

1. What should you do during the opponent’s thinking time?

Evaluate strengths and weaknesses, then plan development and piece improvement.

2. Does every unprotected piece lose material immediately?

No. A loose piece matters tactically only when it can be attacked or captured safely.

3. What makes a piece uncoordinated?

It fails to cooperate effectively with the other pieces, the pawn structure, or the current plan.

4. Why is an isolated pawn often weak?

It cannot be defended by another pawn from an adjacent file, so pieces may have to defend it.

5. Why is king safety connected to coordination?

An attack becomes dangerous when several pieces work together on open lines toward the king.
