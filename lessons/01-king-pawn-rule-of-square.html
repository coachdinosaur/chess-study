King + Pawn vs King · Chapter 1

The Rule of the Square

A beginner-friendly guide to the simplest and most common pawn endgame. Learn when a lone pawn can outrun the enemy king, when it cannot, and how your own king turns a draw into a win.

  • Recognise the “rule of the square” and judge a pawn race at a glance.
  • Know when the defending king must block and when it can simply catch the pawn.
  • Understand opposition and king placement when the pawn reaches the 6th rank.
  • Be able to convert a King + Pawn vs King position into a win, or hold the draw.
Lesson 1

Rule of the Square

When a pawn runs for promotion and the enemy king is far away, you do not need to calculate every move. A simple geometrical trick tells you instantly whether the pawn can promote.

Position 1.1 · White to move

FEN: 6k1/8/8/8/8/8/P7/7K w - - 0 1

White has a pawn on a2 and the kings are on opposite corners. The black king on g8 looks far away — but is it far enough?

Draw an imaginary square whose base runs from the pawn to its promotion square (a2–a8), and whose far side reaches a8–g8. If the defending king can step inside that square on its turn, it catches the pawn. Here it is Black’s turn after White’s first move, so we must count carefully.

Position 1.2 · Black to move (square already drawn)

FEN: 6k1/8/8/8/P7/8/8/7K b - - 0 1

The pawn has reached a4. Now the square shrinks: it runs from a4 to a8 and across to e8. The black king on g8 is outside this smaller square, and it is Black’s move.

Because the king is outside and cannot enter the square in one move, the pawn promotes untouched. The rule converts a long calculation into a one-second glance.

The Rule of the Square

Imagine a square whose base is the path from the pawn to its promotion square, and whose side is the same length toward the enemy king. If the defending king is inside that square (counting its turn to move), it catches the pawn. If it is outside, the pawn runs and promotes.

Memory hook: each time the pawn advances, the square shrinks by one row from the back. A king that was almost in can suddenly be out.

Main line

1.a4! Kf7 2.a5 Ke6 3.a6 Kd6 4.a7 Kc7 5.a8=Q 1-0

Recap

  • Pawn on a2, Black king on g8, White to move → White wins.
  • The king was already outside the shrinking square once the pawn reached a4.
  • No calculation needed: the rule of the square decides it instantly.
Lesson 2

Blocking the King

When the defending king is inside the square, the pawn alone cannot promote. The strong side must use its own king to block the defender’s path and escort the pawn forward.

Position 1.3 · White to move

FEN: 8/4k3/2K5/8/8/1P6/8/8 w - - 0 1

White’s king on c6 sits directly in front of Black’s king on e7, and the pawn is far back on b3. The black king is inside the square, so White cannot just run the pawn.

The winning idea is 1.Kc7! — White steps in front of the black king, taking the opposition and blocking the king’s path toward the b-pawn. Black is forced to retreat while the pawn marches up.

Block with the King

When the defending king is inside the square, your own king must go in front of the enemy king to block its shortest path to the pawn. The pawn then advances behind its king and promotes.

Key principle: the strong king leads, the pawn follows. Never push the pawn past your own king in this kind of position.

Main line

1.Kc7! Ke6 2.b4 Kd5 3.b5 1-0

The black king is held back and can never reach the b-pawn, which promotes comfortably. (The remaining moves are a routine promotion.)

Recap

  • Inside the square, the pawn alone fails — the king must help.
  • 1.Kc7! blocks the defending king and takes the opposition.
  • The strong king leads, the pawn follows: that order is decisive.
Lesson 3

Pawn on the 6th Rank / Opposition

Even a pawn one step from promotion can fail if the supporting king is placed badly. The single most important idea here is opposition: two kings facing each other with one square between them, and the side not on the move is forced to give way.

Position 1.4 · White to move

FEN: 6k1/8/5PK1/8/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1

White’s pawn is on f6, one move from promotion, and the kings face each other on f6’s file neighbours: White king on f6 is beside its pawn; Black king on g8 guards the promotion square f8/f7 area.

Here the outcome hinges entirely on whose turn it is and on opposition. With the king correctly placed in front of its pawn and the opposition in hand, White wins; without the opposition, Black holds the draw by stepping in front of the pawn or stalemating.

The lesson: a pawn near promotion is not an automatic win. King placement and the opposition decide.

Opposition

Two kings face each other with one square in between. The side that has the move is at a disadvantage: it must step aside and let the other king advance. The side without the move “has the opposition” and controls the path.

Rule of thumb: to win with a pawn on the 6th rank, your king must be in front of the pawn and you must have the opposition. Behind the pawn, or without the opposition, it is often only a draw.

Recap

  • A pawn on the 6th rank is close to promotion — but not a guaranteed win.
  • King placement and opposition decide the result.
  • King in front of the pawn plus the opposition → win. Otherwise the draw is real.

Chapter Checklist

  • I can draw the “square” and tell at a glance if a pawn promotes.
  • I know the square shrinks as the pawn advances — a near catch can become a miss.
  • When the king is inside the square, I block with my own king (1.Kc7-style), not the pawn.
  • My strong king leads, the pawn follows — never the other way around.
  • I understand opposition: the side without the move controls the path.
  • I never assume a pawn on the 6th wins automatically — I check king placement and opposition first.
