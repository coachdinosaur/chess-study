Part 1

Defense Is the Search for Resources

Defense is the art of preventing defeat while preserving or creating practical chances. It is not limited to protecting attacked pieces and is not passive suffering dressed in notation.

Neutralize the threat + reduce coordination + create a new problem = practical defense

The best defensive move often changes the nature of the position. It may remove the attacker, exchange queens, block a line, retreat to a better square, create a counterthreat, return material, build a fortress, enter a drawable ending, activate the king, or force repetition.

Do not admire the attack

The defender’s first duty is to identify what the opponent actually threatens. A frightening piece near the king may be less important than the line, square, or supporting piece behind it.

Part 2

Historical Foundations and Defensive Principles

Wilhelm Steinitz

An attack should rest on accumulated advantages. Remove those advantages and the attack may lose its foundation.

Aron Nimzowitsch

Blockade, restraint, prophylaxis, overprotection, and prevention of pawn breaks supplied strategic defensive tools.

Tigran Petrosian

Defense became a whole-board activity: anticipate the plan, exchange the key attacker, close lines, redirect play, or sacrifice the exchange to destroy coordination.

Modern defense

Counterplay, fortress recognition, exact endgame geometry, and accurate material returns are treated as concrete resources.

Identify the real threat

Ask whether mate, material loss, a pawn break, removal of a defender, or occupation of an entry square is genuinely threatened.

Search forcing resources

Checks, captures, queen attacks, tactical blocks, intermediate moves, and perpetual-check ideas deserve priority.

Eliminate the key attacker

Exchanging the piece that controls the mating square, supports the sacrifice, or removes defenders may collapse the attack.

Reduce attacking pieces

Attacks depend on coordination. Exchanges often help when they remove force without creating another weakness.

Create counterplay

Attack the opponent’s king, center, queen, pawn-chain base, back rank, or loose pieces. The threat must be urgent.

Return material when necessary

Give material back to eliminate passed pawns, exchange queens, close lines, activate pieces, or reach a fortress.

Prophylaxis in defense

Prophylaxis means identifying the opponent’s next useful plan before it becomes a direct threat, then preventing, weakening, delaying, or neutralizing it. Ask: If I passed my turn, what would the opponent improve?

Part 3

Defensive Methods and Terminology

MethodDescriptionTypical use
Passive defenseHold essential points without immediate activity.Direct threats leave little freedom.
Active defenseMeet the threat while improving pieces.The defender can challenge the attack.
CounterattackCreate an urgent threat elsewhere.The attacker’s king, queen, center, or back rank is vulnerable.
SimplificationExchange queens or attacking pieces.The attack depends on coordination.
BlockadePlace a piece in front of a passed pawn.The pawn cannot safely be captured but must be stopped.
FortressBuild a setup in which the stronger side cannot progress.Materially worse but geometrically stable positions.
Perpetual checkForce an endless sequence of checks.The stronger king lacks shelter.
StalemateLeave no legal move while not being in check.Severe material disadvantage.
Defensive sacrificeGive material to destroy the attack or reach a draw.Ordinary defense fails.
Transition defenseEnter a known drawable ending.The middlegame is dangerous but the ending holds.

Drawing zone

A geometric setup known to hold with correct defense.

Defensive exchange sacrifice

Giving a rook for a minor piece to destroy coordination, secure squares, or build a fortress.

Counterplay

Active threats that force the opponent to spend time responding.

Resource economy

Use only the force needed for the immediate defensive task so other pieces remain active.

Part 4

Defensive Decision-Making Framework

  1. Stay objective
  2. Identify the real threat
  3. List checks, captures, blocks, and king moves
  4. Identify the key attacker
  5. Check queen and attacker exchanges
  6. Search for counterplay
  7. Consider returning material
  8. Compare resulting endgames
  9. Calculate the strongest continuation
  10. Perform a final blunder check
Part 5

Defense by Game Phase

Opening defense

Determine whether the threat is real, complete development, secure the king, challenge the center, and avoid unnecessary pawn weaknesses.

Middlegame defense

Exchange attackers, control entry squares, neutralize pawn storms, counterattack in the center, close files, and create flight squares.

Endgame defense

Use king activity, opposition, checking distance, blockade, fortress, wrong-bishop rook-pawn draws, opposite-colored bishops, and stalemate.

Psychological defense

A worse position is not automatically lost. Make the opponent prove the win instead of surrendering to the evaluation.

Common errors

Reacting to a fake threat, defending the symptom instead of the source, weakening the king, refusing exchanges or material returns, choosing slow counterplay, overlooking perpetual check or stalemate, and assuming every worse ending is lost.

Part 6

How to Teach the FEN Positions

  1. Load the FEN without showing the solution.
  2. Confirm the side to move.
  3. Require a verbal description before any move.
  4. Ask for the opponent’s real threat.
  5. Require at least two defensive candidates.
  6. Calculate a short line and evaluate it.
  7. Reveal the model only after the idea is explained.
  8. Reset and let the student defend both sides.
Laboratory 1

Defend the Mating Square, Not the Attacked Knight

Learning objective: Identify the real threat in a sharp position and avoid an automatic pawn attack.

Position 1 · White to move

Black’s queen and knight cooperate against h2.

Student task

  1. Which square do the queen and knight attack together?
  2. What happens after h3?
  3. Which move blocks the queen’s diagonal?
  4. Why is chasing the knight less important than securing the king?
g3
h3
Nd5
Reveal the model defense
1.g3!

White blocks the c7-h2 diagonal and protects the mating square. After 1.h3? Black has 1...Nd4!, using the c6-knight as a tactical decoy; careless capture can permit ...Qh2# because the g4-knight protects h2.

Coach explanation

The visible attacker is the knight on g4. The real threat is the battery against h2. Defense begins by tracing the line accurately.

Common error

Reacting to the closest attacker without tracing the queen’s diagonal.

Transfer rule

Defend the mating square before attacking the piece that points toward it.

Laboratory 2

Active Simplification

Learning objective: Reduce attacking potential by improving the pieces and inviting heavy-piece exchanges.

Position 2 · Black to move

Black should centralize and challenge White’s coordination rather than wait.

Student task

  1. Which side benefits from queen exchanges?
  2. Why is passive waiting dangerous?
  3. Which queen move offers simplification?
  4. How can the rook coordinate afterward?
Qe6
h6
Rd7
Reveal the model plan
1...Qe6 2.Qd3 Re8

Black centralizes, reduces tactical vulnerability, improves the rook, and makes exchanges possible on favorable terms.

Coach explanation

Active simplification improves a defender and challenges coordination instead of granting the attacker complete freedom.

Common error

Retreating everything toward the king and waiting.

Transfer rule

A defensive move is strongest when it neutralizes a threat and improves your position.

Laboratory 3

The Wrong-Bishop Fortress

Learning objective: Recognize a theoretical drawing mechanism despite overwhelming material superiority.

Position 3 · White to move

The bishop cannot control the light-square promotion corner.

Student task

  1. What color is h8?
  2. Can the bishop control h8?
  3. Where must Black’s king remain?
  4. Why does the extra bishop not win?
Reveal the drawing mechanism

The position is drawn. Black keeps the king on h8. White’s bishop controls dark squares while h8 is light, so White cannot force the king away and promote.

Coach explanation

This fortress is based on exact geometry. The defender should recognize the drawing zone early and steer toward it.

Common error

Leaving the corner because activity looks attractive.

Transfer rule

Compare the bishop’s color with the rook pawn’s promotion square.

Laboratory 4

Opposite-Colored Bishops as a Defensive Resource

Learning objective: Understand why opposite-colored bishops increase drawing chances in simplified endings.

Position 4 · White to move

Each bishop controls squares the opposing bishop cannot challenge directly.

Student task

  1. Can either bishop directly challenge the other?
  2. Which squares can each side blockade?
  3. What would the stronger side need?
  4. Why is one pawn often insufficient?
Reveal the defensive idea

Without king penetration, a second weakness, or zugzwang, each side can defend squares the opposing bishop cannot contest.

Coach explanation

The defender seeks fixed pawns, blockade squares, king activity, and exchanges that preserve opposite-colored bishops.

Common error

Entering the ending without checking king penetration.

Transfer rule

Opposite-colored bishops often favor the defender after heavy pieces disappear.

Laboratory 5

Blockade Before Counterplay

Learning objective: Hold a dangerous passed pawn before seeking activity elsewhere.

Position 5 · White to move

The knight on d5 performs the essential job of blockading the passed pawn.

Student task

  1. Which piece blockades the pawn?
  2. Why must the knight remain connected to d5?
  3. How can White improve the king?
  4. Calculate the sample maneuver.
Ke4
Nc7
Nf4
Reveal the model line
1.Ke4 Ke6 2.Nf4+

White improves the king while preserving control of the pawn, then uses a forcing check to keep the defensive setup active.

Coach explanation

A blockader is judged by function, not appearance. The knight completely stops the passed pawn.

Common error

Moving the knight away and allowing ...d4.

Transfer rule

Neutralize the passed pawn first; create counterplay only while the blockade remains secure.

Laboratory 6

Stalemate Is a Constructed Resource

Learning objective: Recognize stalemate and understand how defenders deliberately seek it.

Position 6 · Black to move

Black has no legal move, but the king is not in check.

Student task

  1. Is Black in check?
  2. List every adjacent square.
  3. Why is each unavailable?
  4. What is the result?
Reveal the result

Black has no legal move and is not in check. The position is stalemate, so the game is drawn.

Coach explanation

A defender may deliberately give away material, force the king into a corner, and remove every legal move to construct a draw.

Common error

Assuming a bare king is automatically lost.

Transfer rule

When ordinary defense fails, search for perpetual check, repetition, fortress, or stalemate.

Review

Exercises, Chapter Summary, and Mastery Check

Exercise 1: Why can Black hold the wrong-bishop ending?

The bishop cannot control h8, so Black keeps the king in the promotion corner and White cannot force promotion.

Exercise 2: Why are opposite-colored bishops often defensible?

Each bishop can protect or blockade squares the opposing bishop cannot contest directly.

Exercise 3: Passive waiting or active simplification?

Black should centralize and coordinate the heavy pieces so exchanges reduce White’s attacking potential.

Exercise 4: Review one defensive loss.

Identify the real threat, key attacker, possible exchange, counterplay, drawable transition, and the moment you mentally treated the position as lost.

Defending tough positions means

  1. Identify the real threat.
  2. Choose passivity, activity, or counterattack according to the position.
  3. Eliminate the key attacker.
  4. Reduce attacking coordination.
  5. Create counterplay.
  6. Return material when necessary.
  7. Steer toward a drawable construction.
  8. Calculate the strongest response.

The best defenders reorganize the board until the opponent’s advantage becomes difficult or impossible to use.

Mastery standard

  1. Identify the real threat.
  2. Distinguish active from passive defense.
  3. Exchange the correct attacker.
  4. Recognize known drawing geometry.
  5. Continue resisting without inventing a threat that is not there.
