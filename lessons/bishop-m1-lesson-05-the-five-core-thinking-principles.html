The Five Core Thinking Principles

Use five essential questions to examine the position before choosing your move.

Check capturesEvaluate every possible capture and recapture before deciding.
Find weaknesses and threatsScan both positions for vulnerable targets and urgent tactical ideas.
Improve developmentBring inactive pieces into the game and strengthen coordination.
Interpret the last moveDetermine what changed and what the opponent intends to do next.

1

Captures

Question: Are there any pieces that you or your opponent can capture?

Evaluate every possible capture before making a decision. Do not capture automatically.

  • What material will be gained or lost?
  • Can the capturing piece be recaptured?
  • Will the capture create weaknesses?
  • Will the resulting position favor you or your opponent?
2

Weaknesses

Question: Are there any weaknesses in the position?

Look for weak squares, weak files, weak diagonals, weak pawns, damaged pawn structures, inactive pieces, unprotected pieces, and an unsafe king.

Consider weaknesses in both your own position and your opponent’s position.

3

Threats

Question: Do you or your opponent have any threats?

  • What is your opponent threatening immediately?
  • Do you have an immediate tactical threat?
  • Can the opponent create a stronger threat on the next move?
  • Must a threat be stopped immediately?
4

Development of Pieces

Question: Are there any undeveloped pieces?

Try to bring undeveloped pieces into the game and improve pieces that are poorly placed.

A developed piece should contribute to control of the center, king safety, attacking opportunities, defense, and coordination with the other pieces.

5

The Opponent’s Last Move

Question: Why did the opponent make the last move, and what could the opponent’s plan be?

After every opponent move, ask:

  • What changed in the position?
  • What does the moved piece now attack?
  • What did the piece stop defending?
  • Did the move create a tactical threat?
  • Did the move create a weakness?
  • What is the opponent planning next?

Example Position: 1.d4 c5 2.d5

The position below shows all pieces on their original squares except White’s d-pawn on d5 and Black’s c-pawn on c5. White’s last move was probably d4–d5. Black is therefore most likely to move.

Captures

Black should check whether the c5-pawn or another piece can capture, and calculate all recaptures.

Weaknesses

Inspect the advanced d5-pawn, the central dark squares, and the still-undeveloped pieces.

Threats

Ask whether White intends to gain more space or support the advanced pawn.

Development and last move

Black should develop efficiently while understanding why White chose d4–d5.

Remember

This lesson presents a five-question thinking system for every move: examine captures, weaknesses, threats, development, and the opponent’s last move. These questions help a player understand what is happening in the position before choosing a move. Piece values should be considered throughout the process so that tactical and positional decisions remain grounded in accurate material evaluation.

Begin with forcing movesCaptures and threats often require immediate calculation.
Scan both sidesYour own weaknesses and your opponent’s weaknesses are equally important.
Improve the least useful pieceDevelopment is not only moving a piece once; it is making the whole army work together.
Every move changes somethingThe opponent’s last move reveals new attacks, abandoned defenses, weaknesses, or plans.
✓

Review Questions

Open each question to check your understanding.

1. What five questions form the compact thinking checklist?

Captures, weaknesses, threats, development, and the opponent’s last move.

2. Why should a player not capture automatically?

Because the player must calculate material gain or loss, recaptures, resulting weaknesses, and the final position.

3. What kinds of weaknesses should be examined?

Weak squares, files, diagonals, pawns, damaged structures, inactive or unprotected pieces, and an unsafe king.

4. Why is the opponent’s last move important?

It changes attacks, defenses, threats, weaknesses, and possible plans; understanding those changes helps prevent blind responses.

5. What side is most likely to move in the example position, and why?

Black is most likely to move because the highlights indicate that White’s last move was d4–d5.
