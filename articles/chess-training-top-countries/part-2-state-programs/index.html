Staged conquestChina: the engineered takeover

1974The Big Dragon Project conceived — from a standing start
6Chinese women's world champions since Xie Jun in 1991
2023Ding Liren completes the project as world champion

China's rise is the cleanest case of chess success by deliberate design. The game was banned outright for the first eight years of the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) as a bourgeois relic; the country essentially started from zero. In 1974–75, Malaysian patron Tan Chin Nam and Chinese officials conceived the "Big Dragon Project" — an explicit plan, modeled on the USSR's state-run approach, to make China world-class by the end of the century. China joined FIDE in 1975, detached its chess association from the xiangqi association in 1986, and ran a staged campaign: women's individual title first, then team gold, then the open Olympiad, then the men's crown.

The sequence landed almost exactly as drawn. Xie Jun took the women's world championship in 1991, ending 64 years of European dominance; five more Chinese women have held the title since. The women's team began collecting Olympiad golds in 1998. The open team won in Tromsø 2014, then swept both sections in Batumi 2018. In 2023, Ding Liren completed the project by winning the world championship in Astana.

Mechanically, China runs chess through its sports system: sports schools and provincial teams identify children early, a national team trains in centralized camps, and success is rewarded with the same state machinery that produces Olympic medals. Notably — and unlike the United States — the talent pool is entirely home-grown.

Why it works

  • A written plan with staged goals. Women first, then teams, then the open crown — each milestone funded and measured.
  • Full state pipeline. Sports schools → provincial teams → national camps, with coaches, arbiters, and administrators trained alongside players.
  • Soviet methods, imported deliberately. The Big Dragon Project explicitly copied the USSR's spot-early, state-groomed model.
  • Rewards for results. Champions are treated as national athletes — titles, prizes, and prestige included.

National identityArmenia: chess in every classroom

3 MPopulation — the smallest country ever to dominate team chess
3×Olympiad golds: 2006, 2008, 2012 (plus World Team 2011)
2011Chess becomes compulsory for every child, grades 2–4

No country gets more chess success per capita than Armenia. The cultural foundation was laid by Tigran Petrosian, world champion in 1963, whose fame made chess a matter of national pride; today grandmasters are treated like football stars elsewhere, with billboard celebrations, state salaries, and a president who chaired the chess federation.

In 2011 Armenia became the first country in the world to make chess a compulsory school subject — two hours weekly for grades 2–4, across roughly 1,400 schools, backed by about $1.5 million in state funding. Officials framed it as an education policy ("building the future," in GM Smbat Lputian's phrase) as much as a talent program — but the talent-spotting side effect is obvious: in a country that small, putting a board in front of every child is a national scouting network.

Why it works

  • Universal exposure. Every child learns chess; no talent pool goes untapped.
  • Genuine prestige. Champions receive state honors, salaries, and celebrity status — chess is a respected profession, not a hobby.
  • Cultural continuity. Petrosian → Aronian → the next generation: heroes are part of national identity.
  • Team culture. Armenia's Olympiad teams famously outperform their rating sum — cohesion is trained, not incidental.

The paperworkAzerbaijan: chess by presidential decree

2009Signed State Program for Chess Development, 2009–2014
~35,000Children taught across roughly 66 chess schools
3×European Team champions: 2009, 2013, 2017

Azerbaijan is the only FIDE member known to have adopted a formal, signed state program for chess development: a presidential order in May 2009 launched the "State Program for the Development of Chess in Azerbaijan, 2009–2014." It was comprehensive by design — a chess faculty inside the State Academy of Physical Culture, chess classes expanding through schools toward mandatory status in grades 1–4, a network of roughly 66 chess schools serving some 35,000 children, a purpose-built Baku Chess Palace, and a hosting calendar that brought the 2015 World Cup and the 2016 Olympiad to Baku.

The results matched the investment: the men's team won the European Team Championship in 2009, 2013, and 2017, and Shakhriyar Mamedyarov, Teimour Radjabov, and Vugar Hashimov all reached the world top ten. Azerbaijan shows what the Armenian model looks like when the cultural commitment is replaced — or reinforced — by an explicit document, a budget, and a ministry.

Why it works

  • A literal plan. Signed program, defined goals, named ministries, real budget.
  • Infrastructure-first. Chess schools, a chess faculty, and a Chess Palace before the medals.
  • School integration. Mandatory early grades built the base; the elite layer was already strong.
  • Hosting as strategy. World-level events at home kept chess visible and players sharp.

The golden cohortUzbekistan: concentration and a professional federation

17Abdusattorov's age when he led the Olympiad-winning team
11–0Unbeaten through all rounds of the 2022 Olympiad
2026Samarkand hosts the Olympiad — prestige feeding the next cycle

Uzbekistan's 2022 Olympiad gold in Chennai was the tournament's shock: the youngest team in the field — four of five players under 21, led by 17-year-old board one Nodirbek Abdusattorov — went undefeated through eleven rounds under coach Ivan Sokolov. It was less surprising than it looked. The country had taken Olympiad silver in 1992, its first as an independent nation, and had spent years building a professional federation: hiring elite foreign coaching, backing youth events, and treating chess as a flagship sport.

The state makes success tangible. Abdusattorov, who dethroned Carlsen for the 2021 World Rapid title and earned the GM title at 13, received an apartment from the president. After Chennai, each team member was awarded roughly 600 million soum in cash, an honorary national title, and a Chevrolet Equinox — and the country won the right to host the 2026 Olympiad in Samarkand, closing the circle between hosting, prestige, and participation.

Why it works

  • A golden cohort, trained together. Abdusattorov, Yakubboev, Sindarov, and Vokhidov pushed each other up through the junior ranks.
  • Professional federation. Targeted spending on coaching, travel, and events — plus hiring proven foreign trainers.
  • Visible rewards. Cash, cars, titles, and presidential recognition make chess a first-choice ambition for talented kids.
  • Post-Soviet substrate. The Soviet club-and-school chess culture never fully disappeared; the new state rebuilt on top of it.

Four variants of the same bet: the state decides grandmasters are worth manufacturing, writes it into policy or culture, and funds the pipeline from classroom to podium. Part 3 looks at the countries that proved it works without a ministry — and one family that proved it works without a country.
