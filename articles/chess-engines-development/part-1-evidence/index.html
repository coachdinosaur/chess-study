The Evidence: Engines Made Us Stronger — With a Catch

A natural experiment across the Iron Curtain, 11.6 million elite decisions scored move by move, and a controlled-trial literature thin enough to read in an afternoon. What the studies actually found about training with chess computers.

CD Digital Chess September 17, 2026 ~9 min read
01 Evidence 02 Mechanism 03 Protocol

For most of chess history, getting good meant finding someone better than you who was willing to sit down and play. That bottleneck shaped who could become strong and who couldn't — geography decided your ceiling. Then, in 1977, dedicated chess computers went on sale in Western Europe, and the bottleneck began to break. Economists eventually realized the timing had created something rare in social science: a natural experiment.

The natural experimentTwo Europes, one experiment

The strongest causal evidence we have comes from a study that wasn't designed for chess at all. In Training with AI: Evidence from Chess Computers (Strategic Management Journal, 2023), Fabian Gaessler and Henning Piezunka treated the staggered arrival of chess computers as a trial of whether humans can learn from AI. Commercial machines reached Western Europe in 1977; behind the Iron Curtain they barely existed until after 1989. Two populations of tournament players, otherwise comparable, separated by twelve years of access — a difference-in-differences design waiting to happen.

Drawing on more than 19,000 players, roughly 171,000 player-year observations, and over half a million recorded games from 1970–2000, they estimated that access to a chess computer was associated with about 11 extra Elo points of improvement per year. When players from the two regions met across the board, the computer-exposed side won measurably more often than ratings alone predicted — in one specification, a gain of about six percentage points in expected score.

Two details make the finding unusually informative. First, the benefit was largest when the machine was stronger than the player using it — early computers mainly helped club players precisely because grandmasters could still beat them. Second, the benefit was largest where human training partners were scarce. The machine, in other words, was substituting for the thing chess had always rationed: quality opposition.

But the study contains the catch its title hides. Players with computer access played in fewer human tournaments — they substituted machine practice for competitive practice — and when they did face humans, they became less effective at exploiting the mistakes human opponents actually make. A machine that never blunders is a poor rehearsal for punishing someone who does. The artificial training partner was a complement to human practice, not a perfect substitute for it.

The long recordKeeping score of 11.6 million moves

If the natural experiment tells us access mattered, the second landmark study tells us what changed on the board. Bilalić, Graf and Vaci's preregistered analysis (British Journal of Psychology, 2024) took the top twenty players overall, the top twenty juniors, and the top twenty seniors from each year between 1985 and 2021 — more than 11.6 million individual decisions — and scored every one against Stockfish.

The headline is a slow curve, not a step change. Move quality among the elite improved substantially and fairly steadily across four decades. The clearest inflection belonged to the youngest group: leading juniors improved sharply around the late 1990s, exactly when powerful PCs, game databases, the internet, and strong engines all became ordinary equipment. Older elites adapted more gradually.

The surprise is what didn't happen. The late-2010s revolution — AlphaZero, neural-network engines, Stockfish's NNUE evaluation — produced no comparable jump in elite move quality. The authors are careful to say "at least, not yet": by then the top of the human distribution may simply be close to the ceiling of what unaided play can express. And the study is honest about attribution — PCs, databases, coaching and engines diffused together, so no single technology can claim the gains.

Read alongside each other, the two big studies bracket the truth: access to machines clearly can make players stronger, and generations raised with them clearly did get stronger — most sharply at the moment computers became everyday training partners rather than laboratory equipment.

The thin shelfThe controlled trials are surprisingly few

Given how central engines are to modern study, the direct experimental literature is startlingly small. Two recent controlled trials illustrate both the genre and its limits — and they point in opposite directions.

In one, fifty novice players aged 6–17 were split between a "human-centric" curriculum and a "Stockfish-oriented" curriculum for thirteen weeks; the human-centric group improved more across all game phases. It is a genuinely interesting result, but a low-confidence one — the sample was small, the curricula differed in more than engine access, and some of its reported statistics don't withstand recalculation. In the other, sixty Vietnamese university students trained openings with Stockfish against a control group on the standard syllabus; the Stockfish group scored significantly better on opening tests. Again, suggestive rather than decisive.

The contradiction dissolves once you notice the two trials tested different things. "Teaching with an engine" and "learning from an engine" are not the same intervention — and neither is "engine-first instruction" versus "engine-as-verification." The literature has barely begun to separate them. What it has established is the frame for everything that follows: tool capability and instructional design are different variables. A stronger machine does not automatically make a better teacher.

What the evidence supports

  • Access works. The best causal evidence shows engine access raised real tournament performance — roughly +11 Elo a year — not just engine-assisted analysis.
  • Scarcity is the mechanism. The gains were largest where strong human partners were hardest to find; engines substitute for missing opposition.
  • Substitution has a price. Machine practice did not train the skill of exploiting human mistakes — and players traded away human games to get machine time.
  • The biggest gains came early and young. Junior elites jumped in the late-1990s PC era; the neural-engine era has produced no equivalent leap — yet.

The side effectsWhat else changed while we weren't measuring

The engine era's clearest signature may be demographic. According to Chess.com research on title data, the average age of new grandmasters fell from about 30 in the late 1970s to 22.8 in 2020–2024, with a record-low average of 20.9 in 2021. Abhimanyu Mishra became the youngest grandmaster in history at 12 years, 4 months — a record set, not coincidentally, by a generation that has never studied without an engine. Youth, databases, online play and engines matured together; no study can fully separate them, but the direction of the curve is unambiguous.

There is a darker ledger line too. The same accessibility that makes engines free teachers makes them free cheaters. Chess.com's Fair Play team reports closing on the order of 100,000+ accounts per month for engine abuse — a fraction of one percent of users, but an industrial-scale policing problem that simply did not exist before. FIDE now maintains dedicated anti-cheating regulations, device rules, and supervised-online protocols; the engine created both the crime and the forensics.

And one honesty note for anyone who hopes chess study transfers elsewhere: Sala and Gobet's meta-analysis of school chess found only a modest average academic benefit (Hedges' g ≈ 0.34), and nearly every study lacked a proper active control. There is no serious evidence that training with engines makes people generally smarter — the documented gains stay stubbornly inside the sixty-four squares.

So the ledger after fifty years: engines made humans better at chess, fastest where human expertise was scarcest — while quietly under-training the very human skill of exploiting imperfection.
